Help keeping your pet

Struggling to keep your pet? You're not alone.

No judgement here. Most people who give up a pet never wanted to. Keeping animals in their homes through rough times is how shelters empty out, and it's some of the most important work I do.

Tell me what's going on

Food, vet bills, behavior, housing, a bird you're not sure how to care for: send a short message and I'll point you toward help. I read every message, though replies can take a little while.

Send a message

In danger right now? If a person or an animal is in danger, call 911, or your city's animal control for an animal. More on animal abuse below.

Going through something hard yourself? Crisis lines, help if someone at home is hurting you, and mental health and pet loss support are on Caring for Yourself.

Paying for care

Credit cards, loans and payment plans.

When a vet bill can't wait and help from a charity won't cover it, these spread the cost out over time. They aren't financial assistance: you pay back what you borrow, sometimes with interest.

We don't endorse these, and we can't take responsibility for them. We're only sharing what has helped us. Read the terms before you sign up, and ask your vet which ones they accept.

They're in the help list, under “Payment plans and credit”.

Before you sign up

  • Check the vet bill help above first. Grants and low-cost clinics don't have to be paid back.
  • Watch for “no interest if paid in full” offers. If any of the balance is left when the offer ends, you can be charged all the interest from the day you paid the vet.
  • Most of these check your credit, and applying can show up on your credit report.
  • Ask about a payment plan with the vet's office itself. Some will split a bill if you ask.
  • The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found that people charged deferred interest on medical credit cards paid about 23% more than the original bill. Their report is worth a read.

Small local rescues

Rescues around Hampton Roads.

Small groups that take in, foster and rehome animals, many of them run out of volunteers' homes. Most focus on certain animals, so check what each one takes before you reach out.

Please be respectful and aware that rescues are mostly run by volunteers. They cannot help every call or email. YOU too can assist with their support and this is the best way to get help with your situation.

They're in the help list, city by city, under “Small local rescues”.

Spay, neuter and community cats

Low-cost spay and neuter, and help with community cats.

TNR means trap, neuter, return. Community cats are humanely trapped, spayed or neutered, vaccinated, ear-tipped and returned to where they live, so the colony stops growing.

Clinics and TNR groups are in the help list, city by city, under “Spay, neuter and community cats”. More low-cost clinics are under “Vet bills”. Your local SPCA or humane society may be able to help too, like Heritage, Gloucester-Mathews, Portsmouth, Chesapeake or Isle of Wight.

Breed-friendly apartments

Apartments with no breed restrictions.

These apartment communities said on their own websites that they don't restrict dog breeds.

Policies change, so call first. These were checked in October 2026, and they aren't a guarantee. Most still have pet limits, fees, weight limits on upper floors or an approval step. Call the leasing office and get the pet policy in writing before you apply or pay anything.

They're in the help list, city by city, under “Breed-friendly apartments”.

Animal abuse

If an animal is being hurt or neglected.

You don't have to be sure, and you don't have to handle it yourself. Reporting what you saw lets trained officers check on the animal.

Report it

  • If it's happening right now, or a person is in danger, call 911.
  • Otherwise, call your city's animal control. They're the officers who look into cruelty and neglect. In many cities you reach them through the police non-emergency number.
  • Most agencies take reports without your name, but giving it makes it easier for them to follow up.
  • Posting about it online isn't a report. Call, even if you've seen it shared.

What to tell them

  • Where: the address, or the closest landmark.
  • When you saw it, and whether it keeps happening.
  • What you saw: the animal, how it looked, and what was done to it.
  • Photos or video, but only if you can take them safely from a public place, like the street, or your own property.
  • Write it down while it's fresh, with dates and times.
  • Keep a note of who you talked to and when, and check back.

Keep yourself safe

  • Don't confront the person, and don't go onto their property.
  • Don't take the animal yourself, even to help. It can put you in danger, get you in legal trouble and hurt the case.
  • A pet left in a hot car, or out in freezing weather without shelter, is an emergency. Call right away.

How vets help

  • Vets are trained to notice injuries and neglect that don't look like accidents.
  • Virginia law protects vets who report suspected cruelty in good faith, and the state's rules for vets count not reporting it as unprofessional conduct.
  • A vet's exam and records can be the evidence a case needs.
  • If someone has hurt your pet, take them to a vet and ask for a copy of the records.

If you're being hurt too

People who hurt animals often hurt people too. If someone at home is hurting you or threatening your pet, Caring for Yourself has hotlines and help keeping your pet safe while you leave.

Caring for an animal who's been hurt

  • Go slowly. Let them come to you, and give them a quiet space of their own.
  • Fear can look like growling or hiding. It's not a bad dog, it's a scared one.
  • The behavior help above is free or low cost, and it's there for exactly this.

Your city's animal control is in the help list, under “Report animal abuse”.

Wild animals

Found a wild animal? That's different help.

Everything above is for pets. Wild animals need people who are licensed to care for them, and in Virginia it's against the law to keep or raise one yourself, even to help it.

Wild, or someone's pet?

  • Wild: songbirds, hawks and owls, seabirds, wild ducks and geese, squirrels, wild rabbits, raccoons, opossums, foxes, deer, bats, and turtles and snakes found outside.
  • Pets: dogs, cats, parrots, budgies and other cage birds, pet rabbits, ferrets, and farm ducks that can't fly. A lost or dumped pet gets the help above, or your local shelter.
  • Not sure? Call one of the wildlife numbers below and describe it.

Before you touch it

  • Many babies aren't orphans. Their parents leave them alone for hours and come back.
  • Keep pets and children away, and watch from a distance.
  • Don't give it food or water.
  • Never pick up a bat, raccoon, fox, skunk or groundhog with bare hands. They can carry rabies.
